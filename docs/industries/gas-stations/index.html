Gas Station Funding

A tanker delivery can cost tens of thousands before a gallon is sold. Funding helps stations manage fuel, store inventory and upgrades.

Fidelity Funding Editorial TeamUpdated Oct 7, 2026· 6 min read

The fuel supplier needs payment for tomorrow's delivery. Prices rose overnight, so the same gallons cost more than last week, and your customers are not paying more at the pump yet. Between fuel and the c-store, a gas station moves a lot of money every day and keeps very little of it.

Fuel margins are famously thin, often measured in cents per gallon, and card fees take a share of every sale, so a high ticket from a price spike can cost you more in processing than you earned. The convenience store inside carries better margins, which is why many stations push food, drinks and services.

Here is how cash really flows through a station and where outside funding tends to be used.

Key takeaways

  • Fuel margins are thin, so repayment must be tested against net margin, not deposits.
  • Compliance deadlines often force pump and tank upgrades on someone else's schedule.
  • The c-store side usually offers the better margin and growth opportunity.
  • Card fees on high-ticket fuel sales deserve a careful look.

The fuel float

Suppliers commonly require payment by ACH or on short terms, and a full tanker load can run into five figures even at modest prices. When wholesale prices rise, you need more cash per load. When they fall, your inventory in the tank is worth less than you paid for it.

Card sales are settled to your bank a day or two after the sale. That lag is manageable when volume is steady, but on a day when you must pay for a delivery and your deposits have not yet landed, you can be short even though sales are strong.

  • Fuel purchases paid by ACH or on short terms
  • Card processing fees on high-ticket fuel sales
  • C-store inventory including beverages, snacks, tobacco and lottery
  • Pump, canopy, tank-monitoring and POS maintenance
  • Attendant payroll, insurance, environmental compliance and rent

Pumps, tanks and compliance

Dispensers, payment terminals at the pump, canopy lighting and underground tank systems all age. Card-network rules and environmental regulations drive upgrades, and a failed inspection can shut down sales. Compliance deadlines often arrive on someone else's schedule.

These are classic equipment-financing candidates because the assets are tangible and long-lived. Smaller or urgent items, such as replacing a pump display or a payment module, may be handled with working capital. Always check compliance requirements with qualified professionals; Fidelity Funding is a broker and does not provide regulatory advice.

Growing the store side

Because fuel margins are small, many owners focus on the inside store: a food program, a better beer cave, a car wash, ATM and money services. Each is an investment with its own payback timeline. Kitchen equipment and refrigeration take capital, and inventory takes working capital.

Before you invest, consider average basket size and how many fuel customers actually come inside. A modest improvement in inside conversion can matter more than fuel volume.

A hypothetical example

Say you need $40,000 to replace two dispensers and add a payment upgrade ahead of a compliance deadline. A hypothetical equipment financing offer might spread $40,000 over 48 months with total repayment around $50,000, depending on terms. A hypothetical advance of $40,000 at a 1.25 factor rate would mean $50,000 total payback in a much shorter period, with higher periodic payments.

If your station deposits $6,000 a day but keeps only a small margin on fuel, a fast repayment schedule can strain you even though revenue looks large. Judge repayment against net margin, not deposits. All terms vary by funding partner and underwriting.

Processing fees and the high-ticket problem

Percentage-based card fees on a $70 fill-up are material when your fuel margin is a few cents a gallon. Your pricing model, whether interchange-plus or flat, and any cash-discount program you use make a significant difference. Cash-discount and surcharging rules vary by state and card network, so verify before changing your pump pricing.

  1. Calculate net margin per gallon and per store basket.
  2. List required upgrades with deadlines and quotes.
  3. Decide which items suit equipment financing versus working capital.
  4. Pull bank and processing statements for the last several months.
  5. Test repayment against a week of lower fuel volume.

What lenders and partners tend to ask

Expect questions about fuel gallons per month, inside sales, the mix of branded versus unbranded supply, and whether you own or lease the property. Environmental compliance records and tank age often come up. Gather supplier statements and a recent summary of fuel and store revenue. Being organized shows you understand your numbers and can speed the review. Approval and terms always depend on the funding partner and underwriting.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get funding to pay for a fuel delivery?

Some owners use working capital or a line of credit for fuel timing gaps. Availability depends on your deposits, time in business and underwriting. Decisions often come within hours, and funding often within 24 hours once approved, though not guaranteed. Your specialist can discuss which structure suits your supply terms.

Can I finance new pumps or dispensers?

Equipment financing is a common route because the equipment helps secure the deal. Terms depend on your finances and funding partner. Confirm compliance requirements with qualified professionals. Compliance deadlines and quotes are worth bringing to the first call. Timing, cost and repayment pattern are the three things worth weighing for any option.

Do thin fuel margins hurt my chances?

Underwriters look at deposit patterns and cash flow. High deposit volume helps, but they also consider consistency and balances. Size any repayment against your real margin. Provide both fuel and inside sales numbers for a complete picture. Nothing is guaranteed, and every offer should be compared on total cost before you accept.
